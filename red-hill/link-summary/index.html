Tar Heel History - episodes 1 to 8

TH1 / Tar Heel History

Episode 1 | Tar Heel History Episode 1 ↗

Tar Heel History
Caption-based summary

Brian Avery introduces North Carolina naval stores through longleaf and loblolly pines. He compares needles, cones and spring candles, explains resin as part of the tree's defense, and shows resin-rich heartwood surviving after softer wood decays. He connects fatwood with fire starting and the later tar, pitch and turpentine demonstrations.

Why keep it: Start here for the vocabulary and the difference between living-tree resin and resin-rich dead wood. Historical and botanical statements are the presenter's account, not a formulation specification.

TH2 / Tar Heel History

Episode 2 | Tar Heel History Episode 2 ↗

Tar Heel History
Caption-based summary

The episode connects early accounts of Carolina pine resources with shipbuilding demand. Avery shows a worked living-tree face, discusses longleaf forest ecology and fire, then contrasts flowing tar with solidified pitch. He demonstrates fatwood's flammability and explains why old tree hearts were a valuable resource.

Why keep it: Useful visual explanation of tar versus pitch. Biblical references and historical interpretation belong to the presenter; they do not establish that all ancient pitch was pine-derived.

TH3 / Tar Heel History

Episode 3 | Tar Heel History Episode 3 ↗

Tar Heel History
Caption-based summary

Avery describes English demand for naval stores, Swedish supply and the shift toward Carolina production. A model illustrates an earth-covered tar kiln: resinous wood is heated, liquid drains downhill into a collection barrel, and charcoal remains. The final segment returns to longleaf replanting, grasses and resin in heartwood.

Why keep it: Shows the supply-chain story and the distinction between a kiln product and collected raw resin. Do not convert the historical kiln figures into a Red Hill batch forecast.

TH4 / Tar Heel History

Episode 4 | Tar Heel History Episode 4 - Pine Tar ↗

Tar Heel History
Caption-based summary

A smaller demonstration uses an externally heated iron pot, a grate and a collector to illustrate tar production from fatwood. Avery shows the tar runoff and remaining charcoal, then explains historical uses in rope protection, clothing and ship maintenance. He links tar on workers' shoes to his account of the Tar Heel name.

Why keep it: A heritage demonstration, with explicit fire/explosion cautions in the source. The demonstrated tar is not interchangeable with the cleaned resin in the salve recipe.

TH5 / Tar Heel History

Episode 5 | Episode 5 Making pitch from the tar we made last video. ↗

Tar Heel History
Caption-based summary

The episode reviews the labor and hazards of tending tar kilns, gives Avery's interpretation of the Tar Heel nickname and Civil War associations, and demonstrates converting tar to pitch. He discusses charcoal and horse-manure additions in historical pitch, wood treatment and caulking seams. The mixture thickens and solidifies on cooling.

Why keep it: Historical ship-sealing material, not a salve recipe. The presenter's social/Civil War interpretation needs independent historical review before use as public brand history; no ingredient quantities are imported into Red Hill's formula.

TH6 / Tar Heel History

Episode 6 | STARTING A FIRE WITH FAT LIGHTERED ↗

Tar Heel History
Caption-based summary

In snowy weather Avery identifies resin-rich pine heartwood and splits it into small pieces. He lights a small piece and uses it to ignite progressively larger wood in a stove, showing why fat lighter was valued for household heating. He also points out the heavy smoke when too much is used.

Why keep it: Explains a practical use of resin-rich wood. The upload title is unnumbered; its placement as episode 6 follows channel order and a separate course listing, rather than a number printed in the title.

TH7 / Tar Heel History

Episode 7 | Episode 7 - Turpentine Boxes ↗

Tar Heel History
Caption-based summary

Avery explains the old practice of cutting a collection box into the base of a living pine. Display sections show the box and worked face; he discusses seasonal work, wind weakness and fire risk. He then describes Charles Herty's movable cup-and-gutter system, later tools and containers, and log-raft transport to market.

Why keep it: Most useful episode for collection-system history and why movable collectors replaced deep boxes. Historical yield/profit figures are presenter claims, not audited business inputs for Ronnie.

TH8 / Tar Heel History

Episode 8 | Episode 8 Hacking the pines ↗

Tar Heel History
Caption-based summary

The episode starts in the cooper shop with barrel grooves, hoops, leakage and makers' brands. Outdoors, Avery demonstrates renewing a resin-producing face with a hack and using a puller higher up the tree. He distinguishes fresh liquid dip from dried scrape and shows resin reaching the collection cup.

Why keep it: Good context for collection, contamination control and traceability; the historical barrel dimensions are not current packaging specifications.

L007 / Pine and history

Turpentine Industry Documentary from the 1940s ↗

Carrabelle History Museum
Caption-based summary

This period documentary follows gum collection from living southern pines through barrels, distillation, separation into turpentine and rosin, rosin grading and shipment. It ends with industrial and household applications. Its production-share and tree-yield figures describe the film's period, not current conditions.

Why keep it: Useful full-chain history reference. The period narration is promotional and its numerical claims are not modern Red Hill forecasts.

L010 / Craft and business

Col. Littleton: Our Story ↗

Colonel Littleton, Ltd., Inc.
Caption-based summary

Col. Littleton's team describes bringing production in-house to control quality and inventory, building a local workshop culture, checking presentation and treating packaging as the customer's first impression. Handwritten thank-you notes illustrate their personal service approach.

L046 / Pine and history

How To Process and Refine Raw Pine Resin ↗

AppleStump
Caption-based summary

AppleStump collects hardened exudate, heats it, and strains out needles and other debris. The demonstration ends with a smaller amount of cleaned material and shows the resin-coated strainer being burned clean.

Why keep it: Relevant to separating raw collection from usable filtered resin. The source uses casual 'pitch' terminology; heating and straining do not establish cosmetic purity, sterility or a batch yield.

L047 / Pine and history

Making Turpentine from pine sap for the Redwood Violin ↗

The Redwood Violin Project
Caption-based summary

The Redwood Violin Project tests several small still designs to recover a varnish solvent from collected pine exudate. Failed condensation, vapor loss and copper-related discoloration are shown before a more effective design; the maker distinguishes an early volatile fraction from later oily material.

Why keep it: An experimental varnish project with uncertain product identification. It does not give Red Hill a validated turpentine yield, salve ingredient or solvent-production plan.

Engraving and decorative finishes

L048 / Laser and finish

The Key to Engraving Glass on a Diode Laser ↗

ChalkWatts
Caption-based summary

ChalkWatts compares coatings for engraving clear glass with a 10W diode laser after several muddy or failed results. The tutorial covers an absorbing surface layer, power/speed testing, line interval, overscan and image preparation for a clearer frosted appearance.

Why keep it: Relevant to glass experiments. Keep coating choice and settings tied to that diode/glass combination; do not copy them onto the metal lid.

L051 / Laser and finish

Everything You Need to Know About Laser Engraving Glass ↗

JDS Industries
Caption-based summary

JDS demonstrates preparing glassware with an engraving mask, simplifying artwork and reducing concentrated heat. It discusses dithering and resolution, cleanup of loose glass, and an optional sandcarving step after engraving.

Why keep it: The example uses an 80W CO2 Epilog. Its settings must remain attached to that machine and substrate; this differs from diode engraving through an absorbing coating.

L052 / Laser and finish

How to Create INSANE Wooden Inlays With a Laser - Full Tutorial ↗

Make or Break Shop makes reverse wooden inlays using veneer, aluminum tape, inverted artwork, laser preparation, assembly, sanding and shellac. The finished examples show how veneer color and the design's open area affect contrast.

L053 / Laser and finish

New Technique To Make Laser Engraved Color Fill POP With Pigment Powders ↗

Elephant Memories by JoBeMac Studios
Caption-based summary

Elephant Memories demonstrates filling deep wood engravings with color-shifting pigment: clean and seal the wood, add a contrasting base and adhesive, apply powder, touch up missed areas and finish with clear epoxy. The video also discusses sanding and edge cleanup between coats.
